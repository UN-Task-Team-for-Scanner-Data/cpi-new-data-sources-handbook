Product sampling for price index calculation

NSOs can either choose to use all available data from their new data source, or to use a sample of this data. In the first case, all sold items’ prices and quantities in all outlets are used in the price index calculation. Items are typically weighted to reflect their economic importance and an appropriate index formula is used (typically a multilateral index method). No product or outlet sampling is needed in this case. In the second case, a country may choose to sample from these new data sources in order to replace the existing collection. Some considerations on how to choose an appropriate product sample from these data sources are given here.

Product sampling with scanner data

The availability of quantities sold or revenues for each individual product in scanner data provides opportunities for product sampling strategies for price index calculation purposes. For instance, scanner data generally represents a census of the retailer’s outlets and products sold during a given period. The process of filtering these data down to those goods and services within the scope of a Consumer Price Index (CPI) is covered in the section "Classification". Thus, a retailer’s scanner data (once classified) provides a sampling frame for statistical sampling techniques for the selection of outlets and products in the context of the CPI price survey.

The scanner data sampling frame can be used to design a non-judgmental sampling strategy. Probability sampling methods for products selection in the context of scanner data are discussed in Ref. 2. The results suggest the systematic sampling proportional to expenditure as the best performing method with unbiased price change estimates and relatively low standard errors. The cut-off sampling method performed well also. This might indicate that in the case of a small sample size, the exclusion of low-selling products from the price index calculation does not significantly affect the index series. While this result gives support to the use of the cut-off sampling with scanner data, this method may not be appropriate for some product categories in some countries. The cut-off sampling method can be implemented using the revenue share as a measure of the relative importance of products in their product categories (see paragraphs 10.41-10.47 in CPI Manual, Concepts and Methods (2020)). For the maintenance of the sample over time, the revenue share could help identify potential candidate products for replacement or during a sample update.

The Eurostat's HICP - Practical Guide for Processing Supermarket Scanner Data (chapter 8) also discusses two sampling methods for scanner data processing: a static sampling approach and a dynamic sampling approach. These approaches are most commonly used to produce bilateral price index methods, but in some instances the dynamic approach can also be used as a way of preparing the data to feed into multilateral indices, see for example Chapter 5 in Ref. 1.

The static sampling approach is very similar to the traditional field collection sampling of the price survey except that the data collection is done centrally instead of in-store visits by interviewers. A representative sample of store locations can be selected using the store level total revenue calculated over a recent period with a reasonable length and the stores with the largest revenues selected in the sample. Then, within a store and a product category, a representative product sample is selected using sales data from a recent period with a reasonable length, for example the previous six or twelve months. As a selection criteria, one may include products that have been sold in each of the preceding twelve months (stability) and that have the largest revenue shares within their respective product categories (representativeness). A threshold may be set for the cumulative revenue share of the sampled products. Depending on the dispersion of sales within the product category, this threshold could be set at 40%, 50%, 60%, etc. Each month, prices are collected for the sampled products using the scanner data for that month.

Whenever a product is no longer available in the data set in a given month, field collection substitution rules are applied. Also, it may be desirable to replace a product as soon as its monthly sales become so low that it could no longer be seen as representative for its product category. It may also be acceptable to let products exit the market before replacing them in the sample. When a replacement is required, candidate products are identified based on product descriptions and sales. The selected replacement products should be equivalent to the disappeared ones in terms of quality. The previous month price of a replacement product could either be looked for in the previous month scanner data or imputed using a quality adjustment technique, similar to rules in place for the field collection. Sales data may be monitored over time and used to keep the product sample as representative as in the initial month the sample was selected. A simplified version of the static approach consists in a direct replacement of the field collection product sample with a sample of products selected from scanner data for a given retailer, using exactly the same sample of stores. The sample size and the sampled outlets remain the same under the field collection. It should be noted that when used in practice, there is sometimes additional resource requirements to identifying suitable replacements from the scanner data. The process by which replacements are chosen and quality adjustments are made where necessary, can also have a significant impact on the final index output. 

The dynamic sampling approach uses a set of filters and an algorithm to select a matched sample of products for each month and the reference month. The product sample comprises of products that are present in both the current month and the reference month and that represent a significant portion of sales in their respective product categories. The product sample may vary from one month to the next. Given the use of pre-set filters, the entire sampling procedure can be fully automated. However, an automated system cannot necessarily handle product relaunches well in the calculation of price relatives, because the relaunched products cannot be conveniently linked to the old products in an automated manner. Challenges raised by relaunches or other sources of product churn make the dynamic sampling approach unsuitable for product groups with a high turnover rate. The dynamic approach improves the quality of price indexes based on scanner data, compared to field collection price indexes or to the static sampling approach. In view of the low level of manual interventions, it is cost efficient. As a result, when the dynamic sampling approach is used, it is easy to extend the use of scanner data to more retailers. A detailed discussion on the dynamic sampling approach is available in section 8.3 of Eurostat's HICP - Practical Guide for Processing Supermarket Scanner Data .

While National Statistical Offices (NSOs) should attempt to use the full universe of scanner data whenever possible, they may decide to use the static or dynamic sampling method if the capacity of their systems and processing infrastructure is limited. Using a sampling method may represent the first stage of a gradual approach to the use of scanner data in the CPI. When some minimum data processing capacity, skill set and infrastructure become available, the NSO may eventually move to a multilateral index method based on the universe of all available products.

Product sampling with web scraped data

If the NSO follows the targeted approach for scraping with web scraped data (see section on Strategies available for in-house web scraping) then this will already form your product sample.

When bulk web scraped data are available for some product categories from some retailers, they provide opportunities to improve the accuracy of the CPI through enhanced coverage of product dimensions such as time and varieties. However, since there is no sale information in web scraped data, the economic importance of individual products in their respective product categories cannot be estimated. The two sampling approaches previously discussed in the context of scanned data cannot be employed. The CPI Manual, Concepts and Methods (2020) briefly discusses a few considerations for using web-scraped prices in the CPI calculation. For instance, web-scraped data can be used as a replacement for the field collection for entire product categories from one or more retailers. In this case, using price data for all available individual products is the option likely to be chosen. Indeed, it is not clear how a sample of products could be selected given the lack of information on products’ representativeness in their categories. 

Before using web-scraped data from a given retailer in the CPI calculation, it is important to verify if there are any differences between web-listed and in-store list prices (or, ideally, transaction prices) for this retailer. Any available information on possible differences between these two types of prices should be taken into consideration. The goal is to ensure that the web scraped prices used in the CPI calculation reflect in-store prices as much as possible. For example, if a retailer uses a regional pricing strategy, it is important to collect and use appropriate local prices in the price index calculation for the lowest level geographic regions.
